\title{Matched-Budget Triton Tuning: Cheap Signals, Realistic Workloads, and Bounded Recovery}
\author{Project Draft}
\date{April 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
When tuning a GPU kernel in practice, a developer rarely has the time to benchmark every possible schedule. They must decide up front which evidence is worth collecting: cheap signals that can be read straight from the compiled binary, a small amount of runtime profiling, or a full benchmark sweep. This paper studies that trade-off for Triton kernels. We build a tuning framework that treats compile-adjacent signals and bounded profiling as distinct tiers of evidence under a fixed measurement budget, and evaluate it on matrix multiplication and layer normalization kernels on a homogeneous pool of NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. Throughout the paper we quote results as a geometric-mean speedup: a value of $1.03\times$ means the tuned kernel finishes in about $97\%$ of the default runtime, while $0.83\times$ means it runs roughly $20\%$ \emph{slower} than the default. Our main findings are that cheap compile-time signals prune obviously poor schedules decisively and rank the plausible ones weakly; that conveniently aligned workloads flatter the selector compared with representative ones; that profiling helps on some kernels and regimes while hurting on others; and that one reduction-splitting knob that looked promising on a narrow search space collapsed once the space was enlarged, which is why we retire it from the final evaluation. After those retreats, a deliberately conservative selector recovers a small but credible improvement on representative matrix multiplication: a geometric-mean speedup of $1.03\times$ over default settings, compared with $0.81\times$ for an earlier compile-signal-only ranking and $1.00\times$ for a naive random search under the same matched budget. The contribution of the paper is not the headline speedup itself, but the clearer empirical account of what lightweight evidence can and cannot buy once the budget is fixed and the workloads are realistic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

What counts as a win in GPU kernel tuning changes once the measurement budget is fixed and the workloads are representative. A selector that looks strong on convenient aligned shapes, or on a lucky transfer trick inside a narrow search space, is not necessarily useful in practice. This paper takes that constraint seriously. Rather than asking whether Triton autotuning can ever find a fast schedule, we ask whether a small, explicit evidence budget can be spent better than default settings or an equally budgeted random search.

That framing matters because the final positive result we report is deliberately modest: a geometric-mean speedup of $1.03\times$ on representative matrix multiplication. What makes that number credible is the chain of mixed and negative findings behind it. Cheap compile-time signals reject obviously poor schedules decisively, yet they struggle to separate the plausible ones from each other. Realistic workloads expose ranking failures that convenient cases hide. Runtime profiling helps on some kernels and regimes while hurting on others. One reduction-splitting knob collapsed on a larger search space and had to be retired. The final improvement survives those harsher tests rather than replacing them.

\section{Background and Context}

Triton~\citep{tillet2019triton,openai2021triton} provides a practical environment for writing schedule-oriented GPU kernels, but it does not remove the tuning problem. A single kernel still exposes tile sizes, grouping for thread-block launch, the number of warps per block, and the number of pipeline stages used to overlap memory and compute. The best setting of these knobs depends on input shape, register pressure, and the memory system of the specific GPU. Exhaustive autotuning can recover good schedules, but it is often too expensive to treat as the default workflow, which is why matched-budget tuning is important: if only a small amount of benchmarking and profiling is affordable, one has to decide which evidence is worth paying for.

Tuning is also a workload-design problem. On regular, aligned shapes, a selector can look stronger than it really is; on irregular shapes that mirror real transformer workloads, ranking failures that were invisible on aligned cases appear. Runtime profiling, similarly, may be useful for one kernel or regime and add little on another. A useful paper in this setting therefore has to explain not only whether a selector wins, but also what kind of evidence is responsible for the result and why the conclusion changes across workloads.

This paper is narrower than compiler-scale autoscheduling on purpose. We keep the Triton implementation of each kernel fixed, study only a bounded schedule space, and insist that every competing strategy operates under the same measurement budget. That narrower scope is what makes the negative results interpretable rather than incidental.

\section{Goal}

The central question is whether an evidence-aware selector can spend a small fixed measurement budget better than default settings or an equally budgeted random search for Triton kernels. We break this into three concrete sub-questions:
\begin{itemize}
\item Do cheap compile-adjacent signals (register usage, shared-memory footprint, and occupancy estimates) do enough to rank good schedules, or do they mainly prune bad ones?
\item Do representative workloads change the apparent quality of a selector compared to conveniently aligned ones?
\item Can limited runtime profiling, together with conservative selector refinement, recover a credible positive result without hiding the failures that motivated it?
\end{itemize}

\section{Prior Work}

The broad conceptual lineage of this paper comes from schedule search and autoscheduling systems such as Halide, TVM, AutoTVM, Ansor, TensorIR, and MetaSchedule~\citep{ragankelley2012halide,mullapudi2016halide,adams2019halide,chen2018tvm,chen2018learningtensor,zheng2020ansor,zheng2022tensorir,shao2022metaschedule}. These systems established that scheduling choices can dominate end-to-end performance and that searching the space of schedules automatically is a productive direction. Triton itself provides local autotuning interfaces, and PyTorch increasingly treats Triton as part of a larger compilation and autotuning stack~\citep{tritondocsautotune,tritondocsheuristics,pytorchcompile2026,pytorchtriton2026}. External autotuners such as Kernel Tuner, CLTune, and KTT are closer in spirit because they treat kernels as parameterized programs and compare search strategies directly~\citep{vanwerkhoven2019kerneltuner,nugteren2015cltune,petrov2023ktt}. Recent agentic GPU optimization work is broader still, targeting full-kernel synthesis rather than bounded schedule selection~\citep{dai2026cudaagent}.

What is less well covered by prior work is the narrow middle ground this paper targets: a fixed Triton kernel, a bounded schedule space, an explicit measurement budget for every competing strategy, a clean separation between reportable and diagnostic evidence, and a willingness to keep negative results in the main story. That question is not larger than the ones prior autotuning systems address, but it is scientifically cleaner for understanding what lightweight evidence actually buys.

\section{Methodology}

All primary studies in this paper run on a homogeneous pool of NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs spread over two host machines in the same cluster. Matrix multiplication is our primary workload, evaluated on a representative shape set (with irregular aspect ratios and masked edges) that acts as the truth source, and on a conveniently aligned shape set that is kept only as evaluation context. Layer normalization is a secondary workload used to test whether the same tuning strategies generalize across kernels with a different compute-to-memory profile. The Triton implementation of each kernel is held fixed throughout the study; only the bounded schedule knobs change between configurations.

Every comparison is matched-budget. The main selector ladder includes the kernel's default configuration, a naive random search, a selector that ranks candidates using compile-adjacent signals only, variants that additionally consult a small amount of runtime profiling, and a handful of revised selectors that adjust how the compile-adjacent frontier is constructed in response to observed failures. On the final matrix multiplication evaluation surface, every selector is allowed at most 12 end-to-end benchmarks and 4 profiling runs. Repeatability is measured with repeated runs at a fixed random seed; robustness is measured with one run per seed across a small seed sweep. Diagnostic runs may be collected to explain a failure, but they are never counted as superiority evidence on their own.

The evaluation also separates calibration from held-out testing. Selectors may spend their limited budget on calibration shapes, but the headline comparison always uses held-out workloads. That distinction matters: the paper is not asking which strategy can overfit a small calibration set, but whether the evidence ladder generalizes to representative workloads under the same spend.

\section{Observations and Motivation}

The first observation is that compile-adjacent signals are useful for pruning but not sufficient for final ranking. On an expanded representative matrix multiplication surface, naive random search reached a geometric-mean speedup of $1.033\times$ over default settings, while a compile-signal-based ranking reached only $0.827\times$; adding bounded profiling on top recovered only a modest fraction of the gap, to $0.844\times$. The issue is therefore not whether cheap signals help at all, they help decisively for pruning, but that they cannot reliably separate several already-plausible candidates.

The second observation is that workload realism changes the conclusion. Figure~\ref{fig:condensed-aligned} shows that the same selector looks noticeably stronger on conveniently aligned shapes than on representative ones. A selector that looks passable on aligned shapes can still be weak on the workload matrix that better reflects realistic irregular cases.

Layer normalization adds a third motivation. Rather than becoming a second optimization headline, it shows that profiling is regime-dependent. In a small-batch regime, profiling helps only marginally over compile-only ranking. In a large-batch regime, the direction actually reverses, with profiling slightly hurting the selector. That mixed result is what makes the broader narrative more believable: it forces us to describe exactly when profiling pays for itself and when it does not.

Together, these observations shift the paper away from a simple ``did the selector win'' framing. The more interesting question becomes which parts of the evidence ladder remain trustworthy once the workload program is made harder and the search surface is allowed to fail.

\section{Ideas and Solutions}

The project responded to these observations by tightening the evidence hierarchy, not by opening a larger heuristic space. Compile-adjacent signals are consulted first, runtime profiling is treated as a separate bounded tier, and reportable profiling is kept distinct from diagnostic profiling. When an earlier revision that explored a reduction-splitting parameter, controlling how the summation dimension of the matrix product is partitioned across thread blocks, failed badly on an enlarged search space, we did not stretch the claim to keep that knob alive. We used the failure to retire the reduction-splitting parameter from the main matrix multiplication surface, and similarly to retire a weak row-grouping parameter from the main layer normalization surface.

The final positive push is therefore conservative by design. The last selector avoids introducing another broad heuristic; it only corrects how candidates are added to the compile-adjacent frontier on the remaining matrix multiplication surface, so that strong compile-ranked families are preserved and balanced candidates that earlier revisions had missed are re-admitted. The scientific point is not that one more heuristic finally solved the problem, but that bounded corrective action can still recover a useful headline once the transfer failures are understood.

\section{Results}

The evidence ultimately resolves into four short answers.

\paragraph{Cheap signals prune but do not rank.} On the representative matrix multiplication workload, a compile-signal-only ranking reached $0.827\times$ over default settings while a matched-budget random search reached $1.033\times$. Adding a small profiling tier to the same selector recovered only a modest fraction of the gap, to $0.844\times$. The signal is clearly informative enough to reject obviously poor schedules but not informative enough to rank the plausible ones on its own.

\paragraph{Realistic workloads matter.} The same selector reached $0.880\times$ on aligned matrix multiplication and only $0.827\times$ on representative shapes; its profiled variant reached $0.887\times$ and $0.844\times$. A study that evaluated only on aligned shapes would therefore have overstated the selector's real effectiveness. For this reason, all main claims in the paper are drawn from the representative workload, and aligned results are reported only as context.

\paragraph{Layer normalization stays regime-split and secondary.} In the small-batch regime, profiling gained only $0.001$ in geometric-mean speedup over the compile-only selector ($1.011\times$ vs.\ $1.010\times$); in the large-batch regime, the direction reversed ($0.986\times$ profiled vs.\ $1.003\times$ compile-only). A later micro-study of an additional row-grouping parameter did not rescue this story; if anything, it reinforced that the parameter was too weak and noisy to keep on the final evaluation surface.

\paragraph{Transfer failed on the reduction-splitting parameter.} On an enlarged matrix multiplication search space that admitted the reduction-splitting parameter, the selector collapsed: a compile-signal baseline reached $1.032\times$ and random search reached $1.043\times$, but the variant that actively explored the reduction-splitting parameter dropped to $0.161\times$. Ablations of that variant, one using only a corrected frontier, one adding profiling, were essentially indistinguishable at around $0.162\times$, all far below the parent at $0.969\times$. The reduction-splitting parameter was interacting badly with the other knobs in the enlarged space, and no correction we tried recovered the earlier gains. This is the most important negative result in the paper and is the direct justification for removing the reduction-splitting parameter from the final matrix multiplication evaluation.

The final mainline result is positive, but it is intentionally bounded. On the representative matrix multiplication workload, the conservative selector reaches a geometric-mean speedup of $1.029\times$ over default settings, compared with $0.810\times$ for the earlier compile-signal-ranked parent and $1.001\times$ for a naive random search under the same matched budget. The paired ablation shown in Figure~\ref{fig:condensed-transfer} confirms that most of this recovery comes from the frontier correction itself; runtime profiling then contributes only a small additional increment on top of the already-corrected frontier.

We keep this outcome at the ``bounded improvement'' tier rather than claiming universal success. The positive mean result is kept at the ``bounded improvement'' tier because it is stronger on repeated-run stability than on a cross-seed robustness sweep, and that restraint is part of the contribution: the paper only claims what the evidence package clearly supports.

Taken together, the evidence makes the project-level interpretation cleaner. The claim that cheap compile-adjacent signals prune better than they rank is strongly supported. The claim that layer normalization benefits from matched-budget profiling is mixed and bounded. The claim that aligned workloads must be interpreted as context rather than as ground truth stands. The attempt to build a selector around the reduction-splitting parameter was a transfer story rather than a simple success, and the final conservative selector matters precisely because it appears \emph{after} those harsher results rather than instead of them.

\section{Takeaways}

\begin{itemize}
\item Cheap compile-adjacent signals help primarily as pruning evidence, not as a complete ranking solution.
\item Workload realism changes conclusions materially; conveniently aligned shapes are useful context but are not the truth source.
\item Matched-budget runtime profiling is kernel- and regime-dependent rather than uniformly beneficial.
\item Negative results matter. The transfer failure on the reduction-splitting parameter is what justified retiring unstable knob families and made the final mainline result more credible.
\item What worked best in the end was a conservative correction on a better-defined evaluation surface, not a larger selector family.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}

This paper asked whether matched-budget Triton tuning can do better than default settings or naive baselines using cheap compile-adjacent signals and a small amount of runtime profiling. The answer is yes, but only in a narrow and carefully evidenced sense. The most useful lesson is that lightweight evidence is valuable in specific ways: for pruning obviously poor schedules, for exposing workload realism, for showing when profiling helps only conditionally, and for justifying why some schedule families should be retired. Within that disciplined framing, the final conservative selector delivers a small but credible improvement on representative matrix multiplication that is worth reporting precisely because the paper also preserves the limits of the method.

That is the strongest way to read the project as a paper. The final selector matters, but the more durable contribution is the empirical template around it: matched-budget comparisons, representative workload design, regime-aware interpretation of profiling, and a willingness to keep transfer failures inside the main story rather than pushing them into an appendix. Those choices turn a modest final speedup into a more convincing systems result.
