The practical question in Triton tuning is not whether search can find good schedules, but what a small measurement budget can buy. This paper studies that question under matched budget: can cheap compile-adjacent signals and bounded profiling guide schedule selection better than default settings or equally budgeted naive baselines? We present \system, a fixed-kernel tuning framework that separates admissibility checks, compile-adjacent signals, limited reportable profiling, held-out evaluation, and diagnostic failure analysis. We evaluate representative and aligned GEMM workloads as the primary case study and regime-split LayerNorm workloads as a secondary validation case on a homogeneous RTX A6000 pool. Four empirical lessons survive the full study. Cheap signals prune better than they rank. Realistic workloads change conclusions materially, with aligned GEMM flattering the selector relative to representative GEMM. Profiling is kernel- and regime-dependent rather than uniformly helpful. Transfer failures provide useful evidence for retiring unstable knob families rather than stretching the narrative into a false win. On the final non-\texttt{split\_k} GEMM surface, a conservative selector reaches 1.0286 geometric-mean speedup versus default, compared with 0.8101 for the compile-ranked parent and 1.0011 for naive random search under the same matched budget. We therefore present the paper's positive result as bounded, and its broader contribution as a clearer account of when lightweight signal-guided tuning helps, when it fails, and why workload realism changes the answer.
