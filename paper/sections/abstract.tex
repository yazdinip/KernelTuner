Triton exposes a rich schedule space, but exhaustive autotuning is often too expensive to treat as the practical default for everyday kernel development. This paper studies a narrower systems question: under a fixed measurement budget, can cheap compile-adjacent signals and bounded profiling spend that budget better than default settings or naive search baselines? We build \system, a matched-budget tuning framework that records candidate admissibility, cheap resource signals, profiler evidence, held-out runtimes, and the rationale behind selector revisions. We evaluate the system on a homogeneous RTX A6000 pool with representative and aligned GEMM workloads as the primary case study and regime-split LayerNorm workloads as a secondary validation case. The main empirical lessons are that compile-adjacent signals prune better than they rank, aligned GEMM materially overstates selector quality, profiling is kernel- and regime-dependent rather than uniformly helpful, and transfer failures provide useful evidence for retiring unstable knob families. On the final non-\texttt{split\_k} GEMM surface, a conservative selector reaches 1.0286 geometric-mean speedup versus default, compared with 0.8101 for the compile-ranked parent and 1.0011 for naive random search under the same matched budget. The result is deliberately framed as bounded rather than universal. The contribution of the paper is therefore not a claim that Triton tuning is solved, but a clearer account of when lightweight signal-guided tuning helps, when it fails, and why realistic workload design changes the answer.
