Modern GPU kernels often depend more on how their computation is scheduled than on the computation itself: the same matrix multiplication can run several times faster or slower depending on how the work is tiled across thread blocks, how many warps are launched, and how aggressively the compiler overlaps memory and compute. Triton makes these choices explicit, but it leaves the developer to pick among many plausible configurations, and in practice developers cannot afford to benchmark every option. They have to decide, up front, which kinds of evidence are worth paying for before running the kernel at full cost.

This paper studies that trade-off directly. We ask whether cheap signals that can be read immediately after compilation — such as register usage, shared-memory footprint, and occupancy estimates — together with a small amount of runtime profiling, can guide the choice of a good schedule under a fixed measurement budget. To answer this, we build \system, a framework that treats candidate generation, admissibility checks, compile-time signals, limited profiling, and held-out runtime evaluation as distinct stages of an evidence hierarchy, and we evaluate it on matrix multiplication and layer normalization kernels running on a homogeneous pool of NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.

Our findings are mixed in a way we believe is informative. Cheap compile-time signals are effective at rejecting obviously poor schedules but are not reliable enough to rank the remaining plausible candidates on their own. The choice of workload changes the conclusions substantially: the same selector appears much stronger on conveniently aligned shapes than on representative ones with irregular aspect ratios. Runtime profiling helps only in some kernels and regimes rather than uniformly, and one reduction-splitting knob that looked promising in a narrow search space collapsed when we enlarged the space, which in turn convinced us to retire it from the main evaluation. After these retreats, a conservative version of our selector still produces a small but credible improvement on the representative matrix multiplication benchmark: a geometric-mean speedup of $1.03\times$ over default settings, compared with $0.81\times$ for an earlier compile-signal-only ranking and $1.00\times$ for naive random search under the same matched budget. We argue that the main value of this study is less the final speedup than the clearer picture of when lightweight, budget-bounded tuning helps, when it fails, and why realistic workloads must be part of the evaluation from the beginning.
