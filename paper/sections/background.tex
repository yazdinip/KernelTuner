\section{Background and Problem Setting}
\label{sec:background}

This paper treats Triton tuning as a schedule-selection problem, but on a deliberately bounded surface. Halide and TVM-style systems search richer schedule languages and broader compiler representations \citep{ragankelley2012halide,mullapudi2016halide,adams2019halide,chen2018tvm,zheng2020ansor,zheng2022tensorir}. Triton exposes a smaller set of kernel-level meta-parameters instead: tile geometry, reduction depth, grouped launch order, warp count, and pipeline depth for GEMM, and a narrower launch/granularity surface for LayerNorm \citep{tillet2019triton,openai2021triton}. That space is still large enough to create real ranking problems, but small enough that failures can be interpreted mechanistically.

The paper's vocabulary is bottleneck-driven. Compile-adjacent signals such as register count, shared-memory footprint, and occupancy estimate are cheap to obtain and useful for rejecting obviously poor candidates, but they remain proxies for runtime behavior. Limited Tier 1 profiling adds a small amount of runtime evidence, but only when counter availability and attribution are strong enough to remain reportable. Everything else is treated as diagnostic explanation rather than superiority evidence. This distinction matters because the paper is about what lightweight evidence buys under matched budget, not about how many signals can be gathered if the budget boundary disappears.

The workload program matters just as much as the signal tiers. Representative GEMM is the truth source because it includes irregular aspect ratios and masked edges that expose ranking failures. Aligned GEMM is retained precisely because it can make the selector look better than it really is. LayerNorm serves a different role: it tests whether limited profiling behaves differently on a workload with a stronger memory component, and the final evidence shows that this story is regime-split rather than uniformly positive.

This framing also clarifies what the paper is not claiming. We do not compare against broader algorithmic alternatives, compiler-level synthesis, or full-kernel generation. Systems such as CUDA Agent operate on a much broader problem \citep{dai2026cudaagent}. Our question is narrower and more controlled: with the Triton implementation fixed and the measurement budget explicit, when do cheap signals help, when does profiling help, and what should be retired once transfer fails?
