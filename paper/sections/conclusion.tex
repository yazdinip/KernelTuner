\section{Conclusion}

This paper asked whether a bottleneck-aware, schedule-first selector could use cheap compile-adjacent signals and bounded profiling to tune Triton kernels better than default settings or equally budgeted naive baselines. The final answer is yes, but only in a constrained and carefully evidenced sense.

The project delivers one strong primary GEMM story: cheap signals prune better than they rank, aligned workloads overstate selector quality, transfer can fail on expanded spaces, and a guarded final non-\texttt{split\_k} mainline selector recovers the strongest representative GEMM result in the project. It also delivers one bounded secondary LayerNorm story: profiling is regime-dependent, weak in \texttt{small\_batch}, and negative in \texttt{large\_batch} under the current matched-budget setup. Finally, it delivers a meaningful negative-result contribution: the transfer-safe \texttt{split\_k} corrective pass is unsupported, and the resulting keep/drop decisions improve the credibility of the final system rather than weakening it.

Taken together, these results suggest that lightweight signal-guided tuning is most valuable when it is treated as a disciplined evidence problem rather than as a universal autotuning solution. That is the paper's final contribution: not a claim that Triton tuning is solved, but a clear demonstration of where matched-budget bottleneck-aware tuning helps, where it fails, and how to tell the difference.
