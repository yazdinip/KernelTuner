\section{Introduction}

The performance of a GPU program depends on two kinds of decisions: what computation to perform, and how to schedule that computation on the hardware. The same matrix multiplication can run several times faster or slower depending on how its work is tiled across thread blocks, how many warps are launched per block, and how many pipeline stages the compiler overlaps between memory and compute. A long line of work, from Halide's separation of algorithm and schedule through tensor-compiler autotuning systems such as TVM, Ansor, and MetaSchedule, has shown that these scheduling choices can dominate raw algorithmic differences, and that searching the space of schedules automatically is a productive way to extract performance from modern accelerators~\citep{ragankelley2012halide,mullapudi2016halide,adams2019halide,chen2018tvm,zheng2020ansor,chen2018learningtensor,shao2022metaschedule,zheng2022tensorir}. More recent systems go even further and treat GPU optimization as full-kernel synthesis or agentic search over entire CUDA programs~\citep{dai2026cudaagent}.

Triton~\citep{tillet2019triton,openai2021triton} provides a more focused environment for writing and tuning GPU kernels at this level of abstraction. A Triton kernel exposes a small but important set of knobs — tile sizes, grouping for thread-block launch, warp count, pipeline depth, and a few kernel-specific reduction parameters — whose best setting changes with the input shapes and the hardware. Triton also ships with built-in hooks such as \texttt{triton.autotune} and \texttt{triton.heuristics} for sweeping these knobs~\citep{tritondocsautotune,tritondocsheuristics}, and PyTorch increasingly treats Triton as part of a larger compilation and autotuning stack~\citep{pytorchcompile2026,pytorchtriton2026}. The question of how to tune these knobs in practice, under realistic constraints, is what we study in this paper.

Tuning a kernel is rarely a pure search problem in isolation. Developers work under a budget: there is a limit on how many configurations can be benchmarked end-to-end, and a tighter limit on how many can be profiled with detailed hardware counters. Within that budget, they must decide which kinds of evidence are worth paying for. Cheap signals that can be read from a compiled kernel without actually running it — such as register count, shared-memory footprint, and estimated occupancy — are almost free to collect, but they are only proxies for real runtime behavior. Runtime profiling is more faithful, but much more expensive, and its usefulness varies noticeably from kernel to kernel. A good tuning strategy has to spend this evidence budget where it is most informative rather than where it is cheapest to collect.

We therefore study Triton kernel tuning as an evidence-allocation problem rather than as an unconstrained search. Our central question is: given a fixed kernel, a bounded schedule space, and an explicit measurement budget, when do cheap compile-time signals guide the selector to better configurations than default settings or equally budgeted random search, and when do they fail to distinguish schedules that really do differ in runtime? To make this question concrete, we build a tuning framework called \system, which cleanly separates candidate generation, admissibility checks, compile-time signals, a small runtime profiling tier, and held-out benchmark evaluation into distinct stages of an evidence hierarchy. We then evaluate the framework on two representative Triton workloads — matrix multiplication and layer normalization — on a homogeneous pool of NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.

The story we tell is deliberately not a single-number triumph. Along the way, several of our initial intuitions failed in informative ways. Compile-time signals turned out to be much better at pruning obviously poor schedules than at ranking the remaining plausible ones. The workload used for evaluation changed the apparent quality of the selector: a selector that looked strong on conveniently aligned shapes was noticeably weaker on representative shapes with irregular aspect ratios. Runtime profiling helped in some regimes but not others, and one reduction-splitting knob for matrix multiplication that seemed promising in a narrow search space collapsed when the space was enlarged, eventually convincing us to retire it from the final evaluation. Only after these retreats did a conservative version of the selector recover a small but credible improvement on the representative benchmark.

We therefore present this paper as much for what it establishes about the \emph{limits} of lightweight matched-budget tuning as for its final speedup. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An explicit evidence hierarchy for Triton kernel tuning.} \system treats reportable claims and diagnostic observations as distinct, and forces every reported improvement to beat a baseline operating under the same measurement budget.
\item \textbf{A workload-aware empirical study} that contrasts the same selector on conveniently aligned matrix multiplication shapes and on representative irregular shapes, showing that workload realism materially changes which tuning strategies appear to work.
\item \textbf{A characterization of where cheap evidence pays for itself.} Compile-time signals are useful for pruning but unreliable for ranking, and runtime profiling under a bounded budget helps unevenly across kernels and regimes rather than uniformly.
\item \textbf{A transparent account of a transfer failure.} One reduction-splitting knob family succeeded in a narrow search space and collapsed when the space was enlarged; we describe the diagnostic evidence that led us to retire it.
\item \textbf{A final conservative selector} that, on the representative matrix multiplication benchmark, achieves a small but credible improvement over both default settings and equally budgeted random search under matched budget.
\end{itemize}
