\section{Introduction}

Performance-critical GPU kernels depend on schedule choices as much as on the underlying computation. That premise is well established in prior work on schedule search and autoscheduling, from Halide's separation of algorithms and schedules through tensor-compiler autotuning systems such as TVM, Ansor, and MetaSchedule \citep{ragankelley2012halide,mullapudi2016halide,adams2019halide,chen2018tvm,zheng2020ansor,chen2018learningtensor,shao2022metaschedule,zheng2022tensorir}. Recent systems go even farther by treating GPU optimization as full-kernel synthesis or agentic search \citep{dai2026cudaagent}. This paper studies a narrower but still important question.

Triton makes schedule-oriented GPU programming practical, but it does not remove the tuning problem \citep{tillet2019triton,openai2021triton,tritondocsautotune,tritondocsheuristics}. A single Triton kernel still exposes tile geometry, launch structure, warp count, and pipeline depth choices whose best setting changes with workload geometry and hardware bottlenecks. In practice, developers often face a more constrained problem than the literature's richest autoschedulers address: they have a fixed kernel implementation, a bounded schedule space, and only a small amount of benchmark or profiler budget they are willing to spend before moving on. That is the setting this paper takes seriously.

The key scientific issue is not simply whether some search procedure can find a fast configuration. It is whether the evidence used to guide that search is worth paying for under a matched budget. Cheap compile-adjacent signals are easy to obtain, but they may not distinguish among several already-plausible schedules. Profiling counters can be more informative, but they are more expensive and less uniformly trustworthy across kernels and workloads. Workload design matters at the same time: a selector that looks convincing on aligned convenience cases may be much weaker on representative shapes.

We therefore study Triton tuning as an evidence-allocation problem rather than as a black-box benchmark contest. Our framework, \system, records admissible candidates, compile-adjacent signals, bounded profiling, held-out runtimes, and the rationale behind selector revisions. The goal is not to claim a new universal autotuner. The goal is to determine, under explicit matched-budget rules, what lightweight evidence can and cannot buy on bounded Triton schedule surfaces, and to preserve negative results when they are the most informative outcome.

That framing leads to a more credible story than a single headline speedup. The empirical picture that survives the full project is that compile-adjacent signals prune better than they rank, realistic workloads change conclusions materially, profiling is kernel- and regime-dependent rather than uniformly helpful, and transfer failures are scientifically useful because they justify retiring unstable knob families. The final representative-GEMM result is positive, but it is deliberately presented as the endpoint of that process rather than as proof that Triton tuning is solved. On the final non-\texttt{split\_k} mainline, a guarded selector recovers the strongest representative-GEMM result in the study; the importance of that result comes from the negative and mixed outcomes that surround it.

We make four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We present \system, a fixed-kernel matched-budget tuning framework for Triton that cleanly separates reportable evidence from diagnostic evidence and keeps selector decisions auditable.
\item We provide a workload-aware empirical study across representative GEMM, aligned GEMM, and regime-split LayerNorm on a homogeneous RTX A6000 pool.
\item We show that compile-adjacent signals are more reliable for pruning than for ranking, that workload realism changes selector conclusions, and that profiling help is kernel- and regime-dependent.
\item We show that transfer failures can strengthen rather than weaken the final story, and we close with a conservative non-\texttt{split\_k} selector that yields a small but credible representative-GEMM improvement under matched budget.
\end{itemize}
