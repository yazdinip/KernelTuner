\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Our work sits at the intersection of autoscheduling, tensor-compiler autotuning, practical GPU kernel autotuners, and recent agentic GPU optimization. The closest category depends on which level of abstraction one emphasizes, so the paper should be explicit about what it does \emph{not} claim as much as what it does.

\paragraph{Autoscheduling and schedule search.}
The broadest conceptual lineage comes from Halide, which made schedule choice an explicit optimization object rather than an implementation detail intertwined with correctness \citep{ragankelley2012halide}. Subsequent Halide autoschedulers showed that schedule spaces could be searched automatically, including with learned guidance and structured exploration \citep{mullapudi2016halide,adams2019halide}. This line of work establishes the central premise behind our study: schedule selection can dominate performance even when the high-level computation is fixed. Our setting is much narrower, however. We do not search over a rich transformation language. We study a bounded Triton meta-parameter space whose knobs already have direct operational meaning for kernel authors.

\paragraph{Tensor-compiler autotuning and learned cost models.}
TVM, AutoTVM, Ansor, MetaSchedule, and TensorIR push schedule search into richer compiler and IR-level optimization settings \citep{chen2018tvm,chen2018learningtensor,zheng2020ansor,shao2022metaschedule,zheng2022tensorir}. These systems are scientifically adjacent because they also combine structured search with predictive guidance, but their objective is broader than ours. They ask how to optimize tensor programs or compiler schedules in general. We ask whether a much smaller Triton configuration space can be searched effectively with cheap compile-adjacent signals and limited profiling under a matched-budget rule. In that sense, our contribution is not a stronger search algorithm. It is a cleaner empirical study of what limited evidence can and cannot buy on a practical fixed-kernel surface.

\paragraph{Triton-native tuning.}
Triton is the immediate technical context for this paper. Its language and compiler make schedule-oriented GPU programming practical while still exposing performance-critical parameters such as tile sizes, warp counts, and pipeline stages \citep{tillet2019triton,openai2021triton}. Triton also includes built-in tuning hooks such as \texttt{triton.autotune} and \texttt{triton.heuristics} \citep{tritondocsautotune,tritondocsheuristics}. More broadly, PyTorch increasingly treats Triton as part of the compilation and autotuning stack, including autotune-oriented \texttt{torch.compile} modes and user-defined Triton-kernel workflows \citep{pytorchcompile2026,pytorchtriton2026}. This ecosystem is important because it shows that bounded tuning is not merely academic. The open question is whether that tuning can be made cheaper, more interpretable, and more reproducible than straightforward benchmark-driven search. That question is where our work contributes.

\paragraph{External kernel autotuners.}
The practical architecture of \system is closest to external autotuning frameworks such as OpenTuner, Kernel Tuner, CLTune, and the Kernel Tuning Toolkit \citep{ansel2014opentuner,vanwerkhoven2019kerneltuner,nugteren2015cltune,petrov2023ktt}. These systems treat kernels as parameterized programs, generate candidate configurations, validate correctness, benchmark performance, and compare search strategies. Our system follows that tradition more closely than it follows compiler-level autoscheduling. The main difference is scope and evidence discipline: \system specializes to Triton, enforces matched-budget comparisons, separates reportable profiling from diagnostic profiling, and treats failure analysis and keep/drop decisions as first-class outputs rather than side effects of a search benchmark.

\paragraph{Recent agentic GPU optimization.}
Recent work such as CUDA Agent treats GPU optimization as a broader agentic synthesis problem over full CUDA kernels \citep{dai2026cudaagent}. This is valuable context because it shows how quickly the automation frontier has moved. It also sharpens our positioning. We are not claiming to compete with systems that rewrite or synthesize full kernels. Our study keeps the Triton kernel fixed and asks a stricter question: given a bounded schedule space and a fixed budget, can lightweight signals guide selection better than default or naive baselines, and can the resulting failures be explained credibly? That narrower scope is precisely what lets the paper make strong matched-budget and negative-result claims.

\paragraph{Practical ecosystem motivation.}
The surrounding Triton ecosystem suggests that tuning remains an active engineering problem even when native autotuning support exists. Official tutorials present tuning as standard practice for performance-critical kernels, and tools such as \texttt{triton-dejavu} target the overhead and reuse problems around repeated autotuning \citep{ibm2025dejavu}. We treat this ecosystem evidence as motivation rather than as the paper's scientific foundation. It explains why a lightweight, reproducible, artifact-rich tuning framework is still worth studying even in a setting with existing autotune support.

Taken together, the literature suggests a clear niche for this paper. Rich schedule spaces, learned cost models, and full-kernel generation systems all matter, but they are not the only meaningful research targets. There is still value in a narrower Triton configuration study that prioritizes scope discipline, matched-budget fairness, and explanation quality. That is the niche \system is intended to occupy.
