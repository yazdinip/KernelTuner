\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We present the empirical findings in the order in which they shaped the design of the selector. Each stage of the study uncovered a limitation that, in turn, narrowed the claims we were willing to make in the final paper.

\subsection{Cheap signals prune better than they rank}

Our first intention was to rank matrix multiplication candidates directly using compile-adjacent signals: a lightweight selector would order candidates by their predicted quality and the top ones would then be benchmarked. We expected this to comfortably beat random search under the same measurement budget. It did not.

On the representative matrix multiplication workload, a naive random search with the matched benchmark budget reached a geometric-mean speedup of $1.033\times$ over default settings, while the compile-signal-based ranking reached only $0.827\times$. Adding a small amount of runtime profiling to the same selector recovered only a modest fraction of the gap, to $0.844\times$. These numbers are perhaps surprising at first, but they are consistent with a simple interpretation: once obviously poor configurations have been removed, the remaining candidates all look similar to a compile-adjacent scorer. The signal is informative enough to reject losers but too weak to rank the plausible ones. From this point on, we treat compile-adjacent signals as a \emph{pruning} tool rather than as a ranking tool.

\subsection{Aligned shapes flatter the selector}

Our next observation came from contrasting the representative matrix multiplication workload with a conveniently aligned one whose dimensions are powers of two. Figure~\ref{fig:aligned-context} shows the effect. On the aligned workload, the same compile-signal-based selector reached $0.880\times$ and its profiled variant reached $0.887\times$, compared with $0.827\times$ and $0.844\times$ on the representative workload. The selector looks meaningfully stronger on aligned shapes than on representative ones.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figures/generated/figure3_aligned_context.pdf}
\caption{Conveniently aligned matrix multiplication shapes flatter the selector. Three selectors are shown along the horizontal axis: a compile-signal-only ranking, the same ranking with a small runtime profiling tier added, and a naive random search, all under the same matched measurement budget. For each selector, two bars report its geometric-mean speedup over the default configuration: one bar for the representative workload (irregular aspect ratios and masked edges) and one for the conveniently aligned workload (dimensions that are powers of two). The dashed horizontal line marks parity with the default. On representative shapes, the compile-signal ranking and its profiled variant reach $0.83\times$ and $0.84\times$ respectively, while random search reaches $1.03\times$. On aligned shapes, the same two compile-based selectors reach $0.88\times$ and $0.89\times$. The aligned workload is therefore materially easier for the compile-based selector and would overstate its real effectiveness on representative shapes; we treat aligned results only as context throughout the rest of the paper.}
\label{fig:aligned-context}
\end{figure}

The reason is not mysterious. Aligned shapes allow more candidate tile sizes to match the problem dimensions cleanly, which in turn makes ranking errors less costly: even a mediocre choice runs into few masked edges or awkward remainders. A selector validated only on aligned shapes would therefore overstate its real effectiveness. For this reason, all main claims from this point forward are made on the representative workload, and aligned results are retained only as context.

\subsection{Profiling helps unevenly}

We then asked whether a small budget of runtime profiling consistently improves selector quality across kernels and regimes. For layer normalization, it clearly fails to. Splitting the layer normalization evaluation by input regime makes this visible (Figure~\ref{fig:layernorm-regimes}). In a small-batch regime the profiled variant gained only $0.001$ in geometric-mean speedup over the compile-only selector ($1.011\times$ versus $1.010\times$); in a large-batch regime the direction actually reversed, with profiling slightly underperforming compile-only ranking ($0.986\times$ versus $1.003\times$). A later micro-study of an additional row-grouping parameter reinforced rather than altered this conclusion: the parameter was too weak and noisy to remain on the main evaluation surface.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\columnwidth]{figures/generated/figure4_layernorm_regimes.pdf}
\caption{Runtime profiling helps unevenly on layer normalization. Three selectors are shown along the horizontal axis, compile-signal-only ranking, the same ranking with a small profiling tier added, and a revised variant, and two bars per selector report its geometric-mean speedup over the default configuration in a \emph{small-batch} regime (darker bar) and a \emph{large-batch} regime (lighter bar). The dashed horizontal line marks parity with the default. In the small-batch regime, profiling gains only about $0.001$ over compile-only ranking ($1.011\times$ vs.\ $1.010\times$). In the large-batch regime, the direction reverses: profiling drops to $0.986\times$ while compile-only ranking is slightly above parity at $1.003\times$. Layer normalization is therefore reported as a secondary, regime-split result rather than as a second headline in the paper.}

\end{figure}

We therefore report profiling as a bounded rather than universal tool: it can be directionally useful on particular kernels and regimes, but should not be assumed to pay for itself uniformly, even under a modest budget.

\subsection{A promising knob family fails to transfer}

Some of the most informative results in the project came from a knob that failed to survive the full evaluation. On an earlier, narrower search space for matrix multiplication, a selector variant that included a reduction-splitting parameter, which controls how the summation dimension of the matrix product is partitioned across thread blocks, showed a modest improvement. Encouraged by this, we re-evaluated the same family on an enlarged search space that also admitted unusual tile shapes.

On the enlarged space, the selector collapsed. The baseline compile-signal selector on representative matrix multiplication reached $1.032\times$ and random search reached $1.043\times$, but the variant that actively explored the reduction-splitting parameter dropped to $0.161\times$. Ablations of the same variant, one using only the corrected compile-adjacent frontier, one adding runtime profiling, were essentially indistinguishable at around $0.162\times$, all far below the parent selector at $0.969\times$. The reduction-splitting parameter was interacting poorly with the other knobs in the enlarged space, and no correction we tried recovered the earlier gains.

This is the kind of outcome that is easy to hide. We chose not to. A diagnostic breakdown of which schedule families the failing selector preferred on a canonical run converges cleanly on a balanced $128\times128\times32$ tile with a grouping factor of $4$ and the reduction-splitting parameter pinned to its trivial value of $1$; non-trivial splits appear only as dominated alternatives. On the basis of this evidence, we remove the reduction-splitting parameter from the main matrix multiplication evaluation surface. A similar argument applies to the row-grouping parameter in layer normalization, which we also remove from the final surface.

\subsection{A conservative selector recovers a bounded improvement}

After retiring the unstable knob families, we returned to the narrower matrix multiplication surface with a more conservative selector. The only remaining change was to how candidates were added to the compile-adjacent frontier: strong compile-ranked families are preserved, and balanced candidates that earlier revisions had missed are re-admitted into the evaluation set.

Figure~\ref{fig:mainline-headline} shows the outcome on the representative matrix multiplication workload. The conservative selector reaches a geometric-mean speedup of $1.029\times$ over default settings, compared with $0.810\times$ for the earlier compile-signal-ranked parent and $1.001\times$ for a naive random search under the same matched budget. The paired ablation in Figure~\ref{fig:transfer-mainline} shows that most of this recovery comes from the frontier correction itself; runtime profiling then contributes only a small additional increment on top of the already-corrected frontier.

\centering

\caption{Final headline on the representative matrix multiplication workload under matched measurement budget. The horizontal axis labels the matched budget as a benchmark/profile pair (12 end-to-end benchmarks and 4 profiling runs, the budget used for every selector in this comparison). The vertical axis reports the geometric-mean speedup over the kernel's default configuration; the dashed horizontal line marks parity with the default. Four selectors are plotted: the default configuration (fixed at $1.00\times$ by construction), a naive random search ($1.00\times$), an earlier compile-signal-only ranking ($0.81\times$), and the conservative selector with a corrected compile-adjacent frontier and a small runtime profiling tier ($1.03\times$). \label{fig:mainline-headline}

We present this as a deliberately bounded result. On a representative matrix multiplication benchmark, a conservative matched-budget selector built from compile-adjacent pruning, a small profiling tier, and a carefully retired set of unstable knob families produces a small but credible improvement over both default settings and equally budgeted random search. The path to this result runs through several informative failures rather than around them, which is what makes the final number credible.
